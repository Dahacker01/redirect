Task Documentation Workshop

Make the work clear.

Choose a task from school, work, home, volunteering, or a personal goal. Document it so someone else can understand what needs to happen, why it matters, and what completion looks like.

Useful task documentation answers:
  • What needs to be done?
  • Why does it matter?
  • What are the next steps?
  • What does “done” look like?
Practice challenge: Choose a real, non-sensitive task from school, work, a volunteer project, home, or a personal goal. Document it so another person could take the next step without guessing.
Don't include passwords, confidential work information, financial details, or other sensitive personal data.

What makes task documentation useful?

Clear title

Use an action and outcome. “Prepare the weekly budget summary” is more useful than “Budget.”

Purpose and scope

Context helps people understand the goal and avoid doing extra or unrelated work.

Steps or plan

List practical next steps when the work is not self-evident.

Ownership and timing

A task is easier to coordinate when responsibility and timing are clear.

Completion criteria

Describe the result, not just the effort. Make it possible to tell when the work is complete.

Peer review exercise

After documenting your task, swap records with a partner.

  1. Have them read only the task record.
  2. Ask them to explain what needs to happen and why.
  3. Ask what they would do first and how they would know the task was finished.
  4. Revise anything that leaves them guessing.

Good documentation reduces avoidable follow-up questions and makes work easier to hand off.
